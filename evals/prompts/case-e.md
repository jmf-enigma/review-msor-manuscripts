## Manuscript capsule

The manuscript allocates a fixed number of reservable practice booths to requests
from two synthetic membership groups. Let `S_it` equal one when requester `i` is
served on day `t`, and let `bar S_g` be the average of `S_it` over all members of
group `g` and all days in a horizon. The formal theorem proves, for the randomized
policy generated by the model,

`| E[bar S_0] - E[bar S_1] | <= epsilon`.

The proof is internally consistent for this difference between two horizon-level
expected group averages. It does not state a concentration result, a chance
constraint, a daily guarantee, or an individual-level guarantee.

The numerical fairness metric pools `R` simulated replications and reports

`| (1/R) sum_r bar S_0,r - (1/R) sum_r bar S_1,r |`.

Thus the experiment estimates the same difference of expected group averages. It
does not estimate the expected absolute realized gap, the probability of a small
gap, or the distribution of individual service. The tables report the gap but not
the absolute service level of either group. Under an allowed low-capacity instance,
serving almost nobody in either group makes the displayed gap close to zero; the
objective contains no minimum-service or welfare floor.

The abstract and conclusion nevertheless say that the policy ensures that each
requester from both groups receives nearly equal access on every day, with at least
95 percent probability. A managerial paragraph treats the plotted pooled metric as
validation of that statement. No conversion bound connects the theorem to the
stronger language. The application motivation documents a disparity in the
synthetic inputs, but does not explain whether it comes from the booth-allocation
rule, upstream membership schedules, or different request timing.

The allocation algorithm and the stated expectation-level theorem may still be
correct. Fair access, rather than raw throughput, is the paper's declared claim to
fame.
